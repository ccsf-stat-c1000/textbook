The t-Test Statistic

Recall that we were discussing the situation of testing for a mean in the case when σ\sigmaσ is unknown. We’ve seen previously that when σ\sigmaσ is known, the test statistic is z=xˉ−μ0σ/nz=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}z=σ/n​xˉ−μ0​​ (note the σ\sigmaσ in the formula), which follows a normal distribution. But when σ\sigmaσ is unknown, the test statistic in the test for a mean becomes t=xˉ−μ0s/nt=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{s/\sqrt{n}}t=s/n​xˉ−μ0​​ (note the use of s in the formula, in place of the unknown σ)\sigma)σ). Here is where the t distribution arises in the context of a test for a mean, because this statistic (with s in place of σ)\sigma)σ) follows a t distribution.

Notice the only difference between the formula for the z-statistic and the formula for the t-statistic: in the formula for the z-statistic, σ\sigmaσ (the standard deviation of the population) must be known; whereas when σ\sigmaσ isn’t known, s (the standard deviation of the sample data) is used in its place. That’s the change that causes the statistic to be a t-statistic.

Why would this single change (using s in place of σ)\sigma)σ) result in a sampling distribution that is the t distribution instead of the standard normal (Z) distribution? Remember that the t distribution is more appropriate in cases where there is more variability. So why is there more variability when s is used in place of the unknown σ\sigmaσ?

Well, remember that σ\sigmaσ is a parameter (the standard deviation of the population), whose value therefore never changes. In contrast, s (the standard deviation of the sample data) varies from sample to sample, and therefore it’s another source of variation. So using s in place of σ\sigmaσ causes the sampling distribution to be the t distribution because of that extra source of variation:

  • In the formula z=xˉ−μ0σ/nz=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}z=σ/n​xˉ−μ0​​, the only source of variation is the sampling variability of the sample mean Xˉ\bar{X}Xˉ (none of the other terms in the formula vary randomly in a given study).

  • In the formula t=xˉ−μ0s/nt=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{s/\sqrt{n}}t=s/n​xˉ−μ0​​, there are two sources of variation: the sampling variability of the sample mean Xˉ\bar{X}Xˉ, and the sampling variability of the sample standard deviation s.

So, in a test for a mean, if σ\sigmaσ isn’t known, then s is used in place of the unknown σ\sigmaσ, and that results in the test statistic being a t-score.

In fact, the t-score that arises in the context of a test for a mean is a t-score with (n - 1) degrees of freedom. Recall that each t distribution is indexed according to degrees of freedom. Notice that, in the context of a test for a mean, the degrees of freedom depend on the sample size in the study. Remember that we said that higher degrees of freedom indicate that the t distribution is closer to normal. So in the context of a test for the mean, the larger the sample size, the higher the degrees of freedom, and the closer the t distribution is to a standard normal z distribution.

As a result, in the context of a test for a mean, the effect of the t distribution is most important for a study with a relatively small sample size.

We are now done introducing the t distribution. What are the implications of all of this?

  1. The null distribution of our t-test statistic, t=xˉ−μ0s/nt=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{0}}{s/\sqrt{n}}t=s/n​xˉ−μ0​​, is the t distribution with (n - 1) d.f. In other words, when H0H_0H0​ is true (i.e., when μ=μ0\mu=\mu_0μ=μ0​), our test statistic has a t distribution with (n - 1) d.f., and this is the distribution under which we find p-values.

  2. For a large sample size (n), the null distribution of the test statistic is approximately Z, so whether we use t(n - 1) or Z to calculate the p-values should not make a big difference. Here is another practical way to look at this point: if we have a large n, our sample has more information about the population. Therefore, we can expect the sample standard deviation s to be close enough to the population standard deviation σ\sigmaσ that for practical purposes we can use s as the known σ\sigmaσ, and we’re back to the z-test.
